📱 High Threat SMS Phishing

Smishing

SMS phishing ("smishing") uses text messages to trick victims. Attackers impersonate banks, parcel carriers, and government agencies. Mobile users are especially vulnerable due to truncated URLs and smaller screens.

🎣 Phase 1 — The Bait

You just received this text message. Would you tap the link?

9:41 AM ●●● 5G 🔋
🏦
Chase Bank
+1 (800) 935-9935
Today 9:41 AM
CHASE ALERT: Suspicious activity detected on your account ending in 4821. Your debit card has been temporarily blocked.

Verify your identity to restore access immediately:
bit.ly/chase-verify-4821

Reply STOP to opt out.
Delivered

🔍 Phase 2 — The Reveal

This text is a smishing attack. Here are the red flags — and why SMS makes it harder to spot them.

🚩
1. Short URL Hides the Destination
bit.ly/chase-verify-4821 — you have no idea where this actually leads. On mobile, link previews are rarely shown. This link could point to ch4se-login.ru/steal-credentials.
URL Expansion Result
Short URL: bit.ly/chase-verify-4821
Resolves to: http://ch4se-bank-secure-verify.xyz/login?ref=4821
⚠ Not affiliated with Chase Bank. Domain registered 3 days ago.
🚩
2. Caller ID / Sender ID Spoofing
The sender number +1 (800) 935-9935 looks like Chase's real number. SMS sender IDs are trivially spoofed. On iPhone, this message may even appear in your existing Chase conversation thread.
🚩
3. Urgent Language + Account Number Fragment
"ending in 4821" feels personal and specific — but attackers either guess common numbers or obtain partial data from past breaches. The urgency ("temporarily blocked") is designed to override rational thinking.
🚩
4. Legitimate Banks Never Send Login Links via SMS
Real banks use their official app, in-app notifications, or ask you to call the number on the back of your card. They never send a link to a web login in an SMS alert.
🚩
5. "Reply STOP" is a Trap
Replying "STOP" confirms to the attacker that your number is active and being read. This can lead to your number being sold or targeted with more sophisticated attacks.

🛡️ Phase 3 — Defending Against Smishing

  • Never tap links in unsolicited SMS messages, even if they appear to be from your bank.
  • Use a URL expander tool (e.g. checkshorturl.com or urlex.org) to preview short links before visiting.
  • Contact your bank using the number on the back of your card or via their official app — not links in texts.
  • Report smishing attempts to your carrier by forwarding the text to 7726 (SPAM) (US/UK).
  • Never reply to suspicious SMS messages — even "STOP" confirms your number is active.
  • Enable spam filtering on your phone. Both iOS and Android have built-in SMS spam detection.
  • Consider a second-factor authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Authy) instead of SMS-based 2FA, which can be intercepted via SIM swapping.
💡
SMS-Based 2FA Is Vulnerable Too
SIM swapping attacks let attackers convince your carrier to transfer your number to their SIM, giving them your SMS one-time codes. Use app-based or hardware authenticators for sensitive accounts wherever possible.
